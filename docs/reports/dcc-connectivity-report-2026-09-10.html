Connectivity is not stable enough to go live

Across every station and every check in the window, the DCCs and their card readers could not hold a reliable connection. 21.4% of checks passed (both devices reachable), 17 of 32 DCCs never passed a single check, and no station met the 99% uptime bar. The possible solutions to be considered (section 7) are: card readers wired directly to their Raspberry Pi over the same power cable, direct Ethernet for the DCCs (or an external antenna on a USB cable), and the matching live kiosk software update — all of them before any rollout.

Cron task pinging every DCC

A scheduled endpoint pings each active DCC and its card reader concurrently, decides connected only when both answer, writes a row to dcc_connectivity_logs and refreshes dccs.last_status. It must be wired to an external scheduler (systemd timer / crontab / Vercel cron) — see the coverage caveat below.

Status rule: a check is connected only when the DCC (Pi) and the card reader both answer the ping. Anything else is recorded as disconnected.

6 Findings

  1. Neither the DCCs nor the card readers are stable. Overall uptime was 21.4% (1,608 of 7,507 checks passed). The DCC/Pi side answered in only 32.9% of checks and the card readers in 32.8%.
  2. Half the fleet is completely dead from a monitoring point of view. 17 of 32 DCCs recorded 0% uptime — not a single check passed — and 24 of 32 sit below 50%. Only 0 DCC reached ≥99% (target for rollout: all 32).
  3. Failures are mostly shared-path, not single-device. 55.7% of checks had both devices down (4,181 checks), which points to the station uplink / switch / access point / power rather than one faulty peripheral.
  4. The card readers (WiFi) are a distinct weak point. 15 DCCs never once answered from the reader. On DCC017 the Pi answered 96.2% of checks while the reader answered 0.0% — the reader path alone was broken. The same pattern appears on DCC023 (Pi 73.5%, reader 0.0%) and DCC024 (Pi 13.2%, reader 0.0%).
  5. The DCC side is unstable too — it is not only WiFi. 14 DCCs never answered from the Pi side, and on DCC021 the reader answered 60.3% while the Pi answered 0.0%. Power, cabling or switch ports on the cabinet side must be checked as well.
  6. Outages are long, not momentary blips. The longest single outage lasted 7 h 03 m — stations that failed stayed failed for the entire window — and stations averaged 8.6 outage episodes each. Flapping is not the problem; sustained loss of connectivity is.
  7. Ping quality is poor: only 4 of the 2,472 "reachable" results (0.16%) carried a measurable round-trip time, and no reader reply ever recorded a latency. A reply without a round-trip time should not count as reachable, so the reachability figures above are optimistic — real stable connectivity is no better, and possibly worse. Fix this before the next measurement round (recommendation E).
  8. Monitoring coverage itself was incomplete. Checks exist in only 7 of the 24 hours of 10 Sep (29.2%) — first check 10 Sep 2026, 06:36, last 10 Sep 2026, 13:39. The remaining 17 hours of the day are unmeasured, so the true daily figure may differ.

7 Possible solutions to be considered

Options to close the gap found in this window. Each carries a cost or an operational trade-off that has to be accepted before choosing one — none of them is free of change.

A. Card reader: connect it directly to the Raspberry Pi over the same power cable Priority 0

  • The solution: wire each card reader directly to its own Raspberry Pi using the cable that already carries power — one path delivers power and data together, so the reader stops depending on plant WiFi entirely and gets a direct data transfer.
  • What it requires: all 32 card readers must be re-configured to work in this direct mode (addressing, protocol and reader settings) before the link can be used — a fleet-wide change, not a per-station tweak, so it needs a pilot on one station first.
Evidence: readers answered in only 32.8% of checks, 15 DCCs never answered at all, and on several stations the Pi worked while the reader did not.

B. DCC (Raspberry Pi): direct Ethernet, or an antenna with a USB cable Priority 0

  • A possible solution: connect every Raspberry Pi directly to the plant network with an Ethernet cable instead of WiFi, so the station link is no longer shared with the readers or with radio conditions on the floor.
  • Trade-offs to accept: it requires installing cables to all 32 locations in the factory, and it will limit the movement of the DCC — a station becomes tied to its cable run and can no longer be moved around freely.
  • Other possible solution — buy an antenna with a USB cable: fit each Raspberry Pi with an external USB WiFi antenna on a USB extension cable, so the antenna can be positioned outside the metal cabinet and away from the equipment that blocks the signal. Quicker and cheaper than cabling the whole factory and it keeps the DCC mobile, but the station still depends on WiFi and on the access-point coverage, so it is a signal fix rather than a link fix.
Evidence: DCC/Pi reachable in 32.9% of checks; 14 DCCs at 0% on the Pi side.

C. Live kiosk software has to be updated as well Priority 0

  • If solution A and/or B are implemented, the live kiosk software must be updated to work with the new topology — endpoint/address configuration, how it talks to the reader through the Pi, connection handling, reconnection and fallback behaviour.
  • Regression-test the full flow on the kiosk after the change: badge read → card reader → Raspberry Pi → application, including what happens when the link drops and recovers.
  • Update the floor runbook and the DCC dashboard/alerts to match the new wiring, and brief the operators on any new recovery steps.
Why it matters: hardware changes alone will not help if the kiosk software still assumes the old WiFi path — the software and the wiring have to move together.
